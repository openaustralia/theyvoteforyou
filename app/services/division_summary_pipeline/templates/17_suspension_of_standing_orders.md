**Jargon Explainer:** *Standing Orders are the rules that govern the schedule and behaviour of the parliament. Suspending them lets a member do something the rules would otherwise prevent: move a motion without notice, make a statement or table a document after leave was refused, rearrange the day's business, bring on a disallowance motion, or move a censure or a time limit immediately. How many votes it takes depends on how the motion was moved. Without notice it needs an absolute majority of the whole chamber (76 in the House of Representatives, 39 in the Senate); on notice, by leave, or under a standing contingent notice it needs only a majority of those voting, which is why contingent notices are used for most Senate suspensions. The motion usually begins "That so much of the standing orders be suspended as would prevent...", and the words after that are what the suspension is for.*

*This vote decides whether the rules are set aside. It does not decide the underlying issue.*
